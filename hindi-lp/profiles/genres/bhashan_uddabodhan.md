# भाषण / उद्बोधन

## Diagnosis signals

Prose in **one voice, addressed to a listening audience that is present**. First-person plural
throughout — `हम चाहते हैं`, `हमारा स्वप्न` — where a निबंध would say `यह कहा जा सकता है`. An
editorial box almost always names the occasion, the place and the date: *मेदिनीपुर जिला
युवक-सम्मेलन, 29 दिसंबर 1929*. Sentences build in parallel and repeat their openings; the piece
ends by **handing something to the listener** — a task, a dream, a demand.

**Tested before `samvad_nibandh`.** An essay weighs positions; a speech takes one and asks you to
join it. The tell is the audience: an essay has readers, a speech has people in a hall who are
expected to do something afterwards.

A **पत्र** carrying the same one-voice appeal (नेताजी's letter on गृह-उद्योग, कलाम's उद्बोधन
printed as prose) reads under this profile too, with the addressing apparatus of
`yatra_vrittant_patra`.

## Lens

**अपील + दृष्टि + सौंपना** — what is being asked for, the picture of the world it comes from, and
what the speaker puts into the listener's hands.

## Guiding question

*वक्ता श्रोता से आख़िर माँग क्या रहा है?*

## Explanation unit — **one अपील / तर्क-सोपान**

A speech advances in **सोपान** — steps, each one landing a claim and setting up the next. One
सोपान is one topic. The boundary is where the ask changes, not where the paragraph ends.

- **The occasion box is one topic.** Who spoke, to whom, where and when. A speech read without its
  occasion is just opinion; the हॉल full of young people is half the meaning.
- **A list inside one appeal stays one topic.** नेताजी's ideal society — no जातिभेद, नारी मुक्त,
  no अर्थ की विषमता, equal शिक्षा, श्रम की मर्यादा — is one vision stated in five clauses, not
  five topics. Use `concepts[]` for the clauses.
- **The closing handover is its own topic.** Where the speech turns from describing to entrusting
  (`अपना यह स्वप्न वे युवाओं को सौंपना चाहते थे`), that turn is the point of the whole thing.

## Emphasise

- **The rhetoric, named plainly.** Repetition of an opening phrase, the rising list, the direct
  address, the rhetorical question. Name the device and say what it does to a listener — this is
  the one form where craft and persuasion are the same thing.
- **Speech is heard once.** That is why it repeats, why the sentences are long but the clauses
  short, why the vision comes before the demand. Say so; it explains the style.
- **The occasion and the date do work.** 1929, before independence, to young people — every claim
  in the speech is aimed at that room on that day. Read forward from the date, not backward from
  what we now know happened.
- **One voice is the form, not a flaw.** The speaker is not being unfair by not arguing the other
  side. Where the class-8 reader should weigh the claim, put that in the closing question, not in
  a manufactured counter-argument.
- **What the listener is being asked to give up.** Every real appeal has a cost — त्याग, बलिदान,
  effort. Naming the cost is what keeps the teaching from becoming a slogan.

## Avoid (hard gate)

- **Judging it as an essay** for being one-sided. Its avoid-list is not `samvad_nibandh`'s.
- **Turning it into a biography of the speaker.** The speaker's life belongs in one topic, if the
  chapter supplies it; the speech is about what was said.
- **Sloganising.** Repeating the speech's own stirring phrases as the explanation, so the child
  gets the volume without the meaning.
- **Flattening the appeal into "we should all be good citizens."** Say the specific thing that was
  asked for.
- **Anachronistic scoring.** Neither congratulating nor faulting a 1929 speaker for a present-day
  standard; read what it asked of its own listeners.
- **Inventing an audience reaction** the text does not report.

## Priors

**Media** — one image per सोपान, showing **what the appeal describes** — the society being
imagined, the work being proposed — rather than a man at a podium repeated eight times. One
portrait of the speaker, at the occasion topic, is enough.

**अभ्यास** — expect `मेरी समझ से` on what was claimed, `सोच-विचार के लिए` asking the child to weigh
it against today, `भाषा की बात` on the speech's compound and Sanskritic vocabulary
(सर्वांगीण, स्वाधीन, मर्यादा, अकर्मण्य), `पाठ से आगे` projects on freedom fighters, and often a
`साझी समझ` comparing the speech with an appended letter or poem. Where a topic prepares that
comparison, its explanation should already have named the appeal precisely enough to compare.

**Language** — formal, Sanskritised, rhythmic. Gloss generously: the vocabulary is the real
barrier, and unlike a poem's plain words these cannot be guessed from context.

## Recall priors

One `remember` (what was asked for), one `understand` (why that appeal to that audience),
one `evaluate` or `apply` (does the claim hold today / what would you ask of your own listeners).
