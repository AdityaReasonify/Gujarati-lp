# साक्षात्कार

## Diagnosis signals

Prose laid out in two columns: a **speaker's name** on the left, their words on the right. The
names alternate — one or more interviewers, and one person answering throughout. An editorial box
usually opens the chapter, introducing the person being interviewed and saying who is asking.

Unlike a कहानी there is no plot and no narrator; unlike a संस्मरण the remembering is **elicited**
— someone asked. The questions are visible on the page and they shape what gets said.

In मल्हार the interviewers are often **children the reader's age**, named individually (श्रेया,
तनुश्री, माणिक). That is a deliberate editorial choice and it is teachable.

## Lens

**प्रश्न + उत्तर + व्यक्ति** — what was asked, what came back, and the person who emerges from
the pair.

## Guiding question

*किस प्रश्न ने वह बात निकलवाई जो अपने आप कभी न कही जाती?*

## Explanation unit — **one प्रश्न-उत्तर जोड़ा**

A question and its answer are **one** topic. Never separate them: a question without its answer
teaches nothing, and an answer without its question hides why that subject came up at all.

Two consequences worth stating:

- **A long answer stays one topic.** Interview answers wander — बिरजू महाराज answers
  *"कथक की शुरुआत कब हुई?"* with ancient references, then the Lucknow घराना, then the बैरगिया
  नाला anecdote, then a folk पद, then the later घराने. That is one answer to one question and
  therefore one topic. Use the **concepts layer** for its internal turns, exactly as a long सूरदास
  पद does. Do not cut it into five topics.
- **Two questions on the same subject are still two topics**, even when adjacent. The second
  question exists because the first answer did not settle it.

The opening editorial box is its own topic.

## Emphasise

- **The question as an instrument.** Name what each question is doing — asking for a memory,
  for an opinion, for advice, for a fact. The child is being shown how to interview.
- **Who is asking.** When a child asks a master a direct question and gets a serious answer, that
  is worth pointing at. It models that asking is allowed.
- **The answering voice.** An interview preserves how someone actually speaks — asides,
  self-corrections, a quoted line from a relative. Keep those in `original_chunk`; they are the
  person.
- **What the answer contains beyond the answer.** Anecdotes, verse, names of people and places,
  a proverb. Where an answer embeds a पद, teach it as a पद inside the answer, not as decoration.
- **Cross-answer consistency.** A person interviewed at length repeats their values in different
  words. Where two answers rhyme, say so.

## Avoid (hard gate)

- **Splitting a प्रश्न from its उत्तर.**
- **Summarising the answer and dropping the question.** The commonest failure — it turns an
  interview into a biography and loses the entire form.
- **Turning the interviewee into a lesson.** They are a person answering questions, not an
  example of perseverance. Where they say something hard about their own life — बिरजू महाराज on
  the empty jewellery boxes after his father's death — report it plainly and do not moralise it.
- **Rewriting speech into formal prose.** Spoken Hindi has a shape; keep it.
- **Inventing questions that are not on the page**, or attributing a question to the wrong child.

## Priors

**Media** — one image per प्रश्न-उत्तर जोड़ा, showing what the *answer* describes rather than two
people sitting in chairs. Where an answer tells an anecdote, that anecdote is the picture. Photos
of the real person may already exist in the chapter's art; a photograph and an illustration should
not be mixed inside one composite.

**अभ्यास** — expect comprehension per answer, "आप क्या पूछते?", vocabulary from the person's
domain (here: घराना, गंडा, ठुमरी, तबला), and very often a task asking the child to **conduct their
own interview**. Where a topic prepares that task, its explanation should name what makes a good
question — open, specific, and following on from the last answer.

**Language** — spoken register with domain vocabulary. Gloss the domain terms carefully: they are
unfamiliar to almost every child, and unlike literary words they cannot be guessed from context.

## Recall priors

One `remember` (what was asked / what was answered), one `understand` (why the person answered
that way), one `analyze` or `apply` (what the question achieved / what you would ask next).
